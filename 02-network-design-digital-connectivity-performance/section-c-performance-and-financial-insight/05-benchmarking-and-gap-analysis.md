# 5. Benchmarking and Gap Analysis

## Learning objectives

After this topic, you should be able to:

- distinguish internal, competitive, functional, and process benchmarking;
- normalize scope and definitions before comparison;
- calculate a target gap; and
- convert a performance gap into a capability hypothesis.

## Concept in plain English

Benchmarking compares performance or practice to identify a credible improvement opportunity. It is a question generator, not proof that another organization's target or method should be copied.

## Benchmark types

| Type | Comparison |
|---|---|
| Internal | sites, products, regions, or periods within one organization |
| Competitive | organizations serving similar markets |
| Functional | the same function across different industries |
| Process | a comparable end-to-end activity regardless of sector |

## Comparability checklist

- formula and unit;
- start and end event;
- product and customer mix;
- geography and service promise;
- make-to-stock, configure-to-order, or project environment;
- inclusion of subcontracted activity;
- time period and seasonality;
- financial classification and currency; and
- data quality and exclusions.

## Gap calculation

For a measure where higher is better:

$$
\text{Gap}=\text{target}-\text{current}
$$

For perfect-order rate of 91.5% against a 96.0% target, the gap is 4.5 percentage points—not 4.5%.

## Gap-to-action sequence

```mermaid
flowchart LR
    A[Comparable gap] --> B[Driver analysis]
    B --> C[Capability hypothesis]
    C --> D[Pilot or intervention]
    D --> E[Measured result]
    E --> F[Scale, adapt, or stop]
```

## AsterWorks example

The best AsterWorks warehouse has 99.2% pick accuracy while the new region has 97.8%. Before copying the process, the team checks item mix, automation, counting policy, and error definition. It finds that the new region classifies corrected errors differently. Definition alignment reduces the apparent gap before operational work begins.

## Common mistakes

- Comparing percentages with different denominators.
- Treating a benchmark as an entitlement or forecast.
- Copying practice without understanding context.
- Confusing percentage with percentage-point change.
- Choosing only favorable peer comparisons.

## Original knowledge check

Performance rises from 90% to 94%. Is the improvement 4% or 4 percentage points?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

It is **4 percentage points** and approximately **4.44% relative improvement**: `(94-90)/90`.
</details>
