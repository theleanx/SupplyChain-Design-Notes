# 5. Product Life Cycle, Services, and New-Product Implications

## Learning objectives

You should be able to:

- explain the operating logic behind product life cycle, services, and new-product implications;
- apply the lesson to a realistic planning or supply-chain decision; and
- identify the evidence, trade-off, and trigger needed for responsible use.

## Concept in plain English

Demand changes as a product moves through its life cycle. The supply chain should not use the same capacity, inventory, sourcing, pricing, and service assumptions during introduction, growth, maturity, and decline.

## Original matrix — life-cycle stage through two market lenses

| | **Low market share** | **High market share** |
|---|---|---|
| **High market growth** | **Introduction:** invest carefully, learn fast, build availability | **Growth:** scale capacity and availability while protecting quality |
| **Low market growth** | **Decline / weak position:** rationalize, reposition, reinvest selectively, or exit | **Maturity:** defend share, improve cost/dependability, harvest profit |

## Supply-chain implications by stage

| Stage | Typical demand situation | Supply-chain concern |
|---|---|---|
| Introduction | Low volume, high uncertainty | Flexibility; avoid premature fixed capacity and excessive inventory |
| Growth | Rapidly rising demand | Availability, capacity expansion, supplier readiness, quality |
| Maturity | More stable volume, slower growth | Cost, dependability, process discipline, differentiation through service |
| Decline | Falling volume | Phase-out, obsolescence, low-cost availability, spare/support strategy |

A useful commercial/supply-chain shorthand is:

- **Growth:** availability and quality become critical because unmet demand and quality failures can damage adoption.
- **Maturity:** price and dependability become increasingly important as alternatives are well established.
- **Decline:** the challenge is to maintain required availability at a low total cost while avoiding excess and obsolescence.

## Service review

Services such as warranty, repair, training, transportation, returns, technical support, and trade-in programs can affect demand just as much as the physical product.

A service that once differentiated the company may eventually become **standard across the market**. At that point it is still necessary, but it may have shifted from an **order winner** to an **order qualifier**.

## Realistic example — service saturation

NorthStar once differentiated its industrial pumps with a 24-hour remote diagnostics service. Five years later, all major competitors provide comparable monitoring.

The correct response is not automatically to remove the service. Customers may now expect it as a basic requirement. NorthStar should maintain the qualifier while searching for a new differentiator, such as predictive spare-parts recommendations or guaranteed technician response time.

## New-product introduction

A truly new product has little or no historical demand. That makes its introduction especially risky:

```mermaid
flowchart LR
    I[New product idea] --> R[Market research]
    R --> Q[Qualitative demand estimate]
    Q --> C[Capacity and supply scenario]
    C --> P[Phase-in / phase-out plan]
    P --> L[Launch and learn]
    L --> U[Update demand assumptions quickly]
```

For an upgraded product, prior-model demand can provide a starting point, but cannibalization, new features, pricing, and phase-out timing still need explicit treatment.

## Why it matters

Demand evidence, supply risk, inventory exposure, and customer expectations change materially from introduction through decline.

## Decision logic

Assign a lifecycle stage using evidence and connect it to forecast method, capacity posture, inventory policy, service support, and exit decisions. Do not approve the decision until mandatory conditions are feasible and the residual exposure has a named owner.

## Evidence retained through the workflow

Retain stage evidence, demand range, installed base, capacity and inventory exposure, service obligation, and transition gate. Record the decision date and the event or threshold that requires reassessment.

## Applied decision artifact

Use a one-page **product life cycle, services, and new-product implications decision record** with these fields:

- **Decision and boundary:** Assign a lifecycle stage using evidence and connect it to forecast method, capacity posture, inventory policy, service support, and exit decisions.
- **Required evidence:** stage evidence, demand range, installed base, capacity and inventory exposure, service obligation, and transition gate.
- **Expected result:** Demand evidence, supply risk, inventory exposure, and customer expectations change materially from introduction through decline.
- **Balancing condition:** Early capacity protects growth but risks stranded investment; late-life inventory protects service but increases obsolescence.
- **Accountability:** name the decision owner, approval date, residual exposure owner, and measurable trigger for reassessment.

The record is complete only when another practitioner can reproduce the reasoning and identify the next action without relying on meeting memory.

## Trade-offs

Early capacity protects growth but risks stranded investment; late-life inventory protects service but increases obsolescence.

## Commonly confused with

Do not confuse a preferred decision method with a guaranteed outcome. Assign a lifecycle stage using evidence and connect it to forecast method, capacity posture, inventory policy, service support, and exit decisions. Validate the result with stage evidence, demand range, installed base, capacity and inventory exposure, service obligation, and transition gate; the evidence, not the method's label, determines whether the choice worked.

## Common mistakes
Do not forecast a completely new product as if mature historical data exists. Introduction-stage planning requires more judgment and explicit risk tolerance.

## Original knowledge check

**Question.** NorthStar is deciding how to apply product life cycle, services, and new-product implications. Which proposal is most defensible?

A. Use product life cycle, services, and new-product implications as a label for the preferred option before defining the decision outcome, constraints, or owner.
B. Assign a lifecycle stage using evidence and connect it to forecast method, capacity posture, inventory policy, service support, and exit decisions.
C. Choose the apparent upside without evaluating this balancing condition: Early capacity protects growth but risks stranded investment; late-life inventory protects service but increases obsolescence.
D. Approve the choice without retaining stage evidence, demand range, installed base, capacity and inventory exposure, service obligation, and transition gate; omit the reassessment trigger as well.

<details>
<summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

### Correct answer

**B. Assign a lifecycle stage using evidence and connect it to forecast method, capacity posture, inventory policy, service support, and exit decisions.**

### Why it is correct

Demand evidence, supply risk, inventory exposure, and customer expectations change materially from introduction through decline. The recommended action connects the operating choice to evidence, ownership, and a measurable outcome.

### Why the other answers are wrong

- **A** reverses the decision sequence. The team should first do the required work: assign a lifecycle stage using evidence and connect it to forecast method, capacity posture, inventory policy, service support, and exit decisions.
- **C** optimizes one visible result and omits the balancing effects: early capacity protects growth but risks stranded investment; late-life inventory protects service but increases obsolescence.
- **D** leaves the approval unauditable. A reviewer would be missing stage evidence, demand range, installed base, capacity and inventory exposure, service obligation, and transition gate, as well as the trigger needed to revisit the decision when conditions change.

Those approaches ignore the governing trade-off: early capacity protects growth but risks stranded investment; late-life inventory protects service but increases obsolescence.

</details>

## Practitioner perspective

A review should be able to reconstruct the choice from stage evidence, demand range, installed base, capacity and inventory exposure, service obligation, and transition gate. If those records cannot explain what changed, who decided, and when the decision will be revisited, the process is not yet controlled.
