# 5. Product Life Cycle, Services, and New-Product Implications

## Concept in plain English

Demand changes as a product moves through its life cycle. The supply chain should not use the same capacity, inventory, sourcing, pricing, and service assumptions during introduction, growth, maturity, and decline.

## Original matrix — life-cycle stage through two market lenses

| | **Low market share** | **High market share** |
|---|---|---|
| **High market growth** | **Introduction:** invest carefully, learn fast, build availability | **Growth:** scale capacity and availability while protecting quality |
| **Low market growth** | **Decline / weak position:** rationalize, reposition, reinvest selectively, or exit | **Maturity:** defend share, improve cost/dependability, harvest profit |

## Supply-chain implications by stage

| Stage | Typical demand situation | Supply-chain concern |
|---|---|---|
| Introduction | Low volume, high uncertainty | Flexibility; avoid premature fixed capacity and excessive inventory |
| Growth | Rapidly rising demand | Availability, capacity expansion, supplier readiness, quality |
| Maturity | More stable volume, slower growth | Cost, dependability, process discipline, differentiation through service |
| Decline | Falling volume | Phase-out, obsolescence, low-cost availability, spare/support strategy |

A useful commercial/supply-chain shorthand is:

- **Growth:** availability and quality become critical because unmet demand and quality failures can damage adoption.
- **Maturity:** price and dependability become increasingly important as alternatives are well established.
- **Decline:** the challenge is to maintain required availability at a low total cost while avoiding excess and obsolescence.

## Service review

Services such as warranty, repair, training, transportation, returns, technical support, and trade-in programs can affect demand just as much as the physical product.

A service that once differentiated the company may eventually become **standard across the market**. At that point it is still necessary, but it may have shifted from an **order winner** to an **order qualifier**.

## Realistic example — service saturation

NorthStar once differentiated its industrial pumps with a 24-hour remote diagnostics service. Five years later, all major competitors provide comparable monitoring.

The correct response is not automatically to remove the service. Customers may now expect it as a basic requirement. NorthStar should maintain the qualifier while searching for a new differentiator, such as predictive spare-parts recommendations or guaranteed technician response time.

## New-product introduction

A truly new product has little or no historical demand. That makes its introduction especially risky:

```mermaid
flowchart LR
    I[New product idea] --> R[Market research]
    R --> Q[Qualitative demand estimate]
    Q --> C[Capacity and supply scenario]
    C --> P[Phase-in / phase-out plan]
    P --> L[Launch and learn]
    L --> U[Update demand assumptions quickly]
```

For an upgraded product, prior-model demand can provide a starting point, but cannibalization, new features, pricing, and phase-out timing still need explicit treatment.

## Common mistake

Do not forecast a completely new product as if mature historical data exists. Introduction-stage planning requires more judgment and explicit risk tolerance.
